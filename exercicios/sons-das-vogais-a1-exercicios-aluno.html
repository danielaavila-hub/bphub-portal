Os Sons das Vogais
How Brazilians actually pronounce them

Portuguese spelling and Portuguese pronunciation are not always the same thing. In this material, you will learn four sound patterns that will help you understand spoken Brazilian Portuguese much better — and sound more natural yourself.

Pronunciation Notes

Read this before doing the exercises. Try to say the examples out loud.

1. Final -e and -o don’t sound like "e" and "o"

At the end of a word, when -e and -o are unstressed (not the strong syllable), Brazilians pronounce them like [i] and [u] — even though the spelling doesn’t change.

WrittenSounds likeWrittenSounds like
leiteLAY-tchigatoGA-tu
tardeTAR-djilivroLI-vru
noiteNOY-tchiobrigadoo-bri-GA-du
genteJEN-tchipássaroPA-sa-ru
Good to know: this same rule applies to the little words o / os (the) and e (and) — they also sound like [u] and [i]!
o gato e os ratos sounds like u GA-tu i us HA-tus — "the cat and the rats".
Watch out for the exception: when the last syllable of the word carries a written accent (like é, ê, ó, ô), the vowel is stressed — and stressed vowels do not reduce. They keep their full, strong sound.
café, você, avô, avó, robô, bebê, jacaré — all of these end in a full, clear [e] or [o] sound, never [i] or [u]!

2. Vogais nasais — nasal vowels

When a vowel is followed by m, n, or nh, it becomes a nasal sound — the air passes through your nose, a bit like the "on" in the French word "bon". Portuguese has five nasal vowel sounds: ã, ẽ, ĩ, õ, ũ.

SoundExamples
ãcampo, manhã, lâmpada
ẽtempo, venha, extremo
ĩlindo, vinho, assim
õcompra, ponte, bom
ũmundo, unha, atum

Nasal vowels serve to distinguish words — it’s important to hear the difference:

Oral (no nasal sound)Nasal
grade (fence)grande (big)
mito (myth)minto (I lie)
bode (goat)bonde (tram)
mudo (mute)mundo (world)
Good to know: the word-final spelling -ão (like in não, pão, coração) is also a nasal sound — it sounds like ãu, said through the nose.

3. The reduced diphthongs: ei → [e] and ou → [o]

Some vowel combinations (diphthongs) are simplified in everyday speech — the spelling keeps two letters, but Brazilians often say just one sound.

PatternRuleExamples
eibefore j, r, x → sounds like [e]beijo → BE-zhu, dinheiro → di-NHE-ru, brasileiro → bra-zi-LE-ru
oualmost always → sounds like [o]outro → O-tru, pouco → PO-ku, chegou → she-GO
Good to know: the spelling never changes! You still write dinheiro and pouco — only the pronunciation is simplified.

4. The little extra [i] sound

Brazilian Portuguese doesn’t like putting two consonants together with no vowel between them. So speakers often insert a light [i] sound to separate them.

WrittenSounds like
advogadoa-dji-vo-GA-du
admitira-dji-mi-TCHIR
observaro-bi-ser-VAH
ritmoHI-tchi-mu

The same thing happens at the beginning of words that start with es- or ex- + consonant — an [i] sound appears before the "s":

WrittenSounds like
escolais-KO-la
estadois-TA-du
exposiçãois-po-zi-SÃU

🎙️ How to practice pronunciation with this material

This material is about sounds, so reading silently is not enough — your teacher needs to hear you. For every exercise marked with 🎙️, please:

1. Open your phone's voice memo app (or the microphone in WhatsApp) and record yourself reading the sentences or words out loud, slowly and clearly.
2. Listen to your own recording once before sending — do the nasal sounds and final vowels sound close to the examples?
3. Send the audio file to your teacher on WhatsApp, or attach it to an email, together with your written answers below. (This page cannot receive audio files directly.)

Tip: record the same sentence twice — once slowly, once at normal speed — so your teacher can hear how the sounds change with speed, and give you specific feedback on the patterns above.
