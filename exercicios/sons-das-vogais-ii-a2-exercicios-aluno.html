Os Sons das Vogais II
Deeper patterns in Brazilian pronunciation

This is a follow-up to "Os Sons das Vogais" (A1). Here you will learn five more patterns: how nouns and verbs with the same spelling can sound different, when nasal sounds turn off, how words link together in fast speech, and how -ão and -am can even tell you which verb tense you are hearing.

Pronunciation Notes

Read this before doing the exercises. Try to say the examples out loud.

1. Nouns vs. verbs: the same letters, a different vowel

Some Brazilian words are spelled with e or o, but the noun and the verb that share that spelling are pronounced with a different vowel quality. The noun uses a closed vowel ([e], [o]); the verb uses an open vowel ([ɛ], [ɔ]) — a bit more like the "e" in "bed" or the "o" in "hot".

Noun (closed)Verb (open)
o acertoeu acerto (open ɛ)
o começoeu começo (open ɛ)
o almoçoeu almoço (open ɔ)
o jogoeu jogo (open ɔ)
o olhoeu olho (open ɔ)
Good to know: the same closed/open alternation also shows up between singular and plural nouns! In the singular, the stressed o is closed; in the plural, it often opens up.
o ovo (closed) → os ovos (open)  ·  o olho (closed) → os olhos (open)  ·  o jogo (closed) → os jogos (open)

2. -or endings: usually closed, with famous exceptions

Words ending in -or are normally pronounced with a closed [o]: amor, motor, professor, cor. But a small group of very common comparative words break the rule and use an open [ɔ] instead.

Closed [o] — the ruleOpen [ɔ] — the exceptions
amor, calor, motormaior, melhor, pior
professor, elevadormenor, suor
Memory tip: the exceptions are almost all comparative words — maior (bigger), melhor (better), pior (worse), menor (smaller). If a word means "more/less ___", it probably has the open sound!

3. When nasalization turns off

You learned that m and n nasalize the vowel right before them. But there is an important condition: this only happens when the m/n is followed by another consonant (or is at the end of the word). If the m/n is followed by a vowel, it stays a normal, oral consonant — and the vowel before it is not nasalized.

Nasalized (m/n + consonant)NOT nasalized (m/n + vowel)
campo, sombracanal, acomodar
infância, cincosinal, banana (1st a)
Compare: canal — the a before n is oral, because n is followed by the vowel a. But in campo, the a before m IS nasal, because m is followed by the consonant p.

4. The invisible link between words

When a word ends in a nasal vowel and the next word starts with a vowel, Brazilians often connect them with a light [ɲ] sound (like the "nh" in "sonho"). This is called ligação — word linking.

Linking happensNo linking
sim ou nãocom ela
bem aquibom amigo
também euum ovo
The rule: linking happens after words ending in -im, -em/-ém, -en. It does not happen after words ending in -um, -om, or -ã — those simply pause before the next vowel.

5. Two more diphthong patterns

The diphthong ai before x: just like ei simplifies before j/r/x, the diphthong ai reduces to a single [a] sound when it comes before an x.

WrittenSounds like
baixoBA-shu
caixaKA-sha
faixaFA-sha

The extra [i] before final -s/-z: when a stressed vowel is the last sound of a word and it is followed by -s or -z, Brazilians often turn it into a diphthong by adding a light [i] sound.

WrittenSounds like
nósNOIS
trêsTREIS
vocêsvo-SEIS
arroza-HOIS

6. -ão or -am? A sound that reveals a verb tense

-ão and -am are written differently, but both are pronounced the same way: [ãu]. So how do you know which one a verb ending in this sound uses? It depends on the tense!

TenseEndingExample
Future (eles/elas)-ão (stressed)eles falarão, sairão
Other tenses (eles/elas)-am (unstressed)eles falavam, cantaram
Bonus pattern: the same kind of written accent trick happens with -ém/-êm: ele convém (singular) vs. eles convêm (plural) — same sound [ẽi], different grammar, told apart only by the accent.
